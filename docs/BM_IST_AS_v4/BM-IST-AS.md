# Invariants, Principles, and Evals: A Framework for TOE Candidate Survival

## Part 1: Invariants from Chaos and Complexity Theory

Chaos theory and complexity theory are the two mature mathematical disciplines that study how simple rules produce complex behavior, and how complex behavior simplifies into universal patterns. A TOE candidate must respect their findings, because these are invariants observed across every physical system we can measure.

### 1.1 The invariant list

**From chaos theory:**

**I1 — Sensitive dependence (positive Lyapunov exponents where present).** If the substrate is chaotic, nearby states diverge exponentially. A TOE must either explain why this divergence is bounded by a finite attractor (as in dissipative chaos) or explain why the relevant degrees of freedom are not chaotic.

**I2 — Invariant measures.** A chaotic system does not have a single trajectory but a distribution. The distribution is not arbitrary — it is the fixed point of the transfer operator. A TOE must specify which measure is physical and why.

**I3 — Attractor structure.** Real systems do not wander through all of state space. They settle onto attractors: fixed points, limit cycles, tori, strange attractors. A TOE must identify the attractors of its substrate dynamics and show they correspond to physical states.

**I4 — Structural stability.** A physically realized system must survive small perturbations of its parameters. A TOE whose predictions depend on exact values of its constants (without a mechanism fixing them) fails this invariant.

**I5 — Universality classes.** Different microscopic systems flow to the same macroscopic fixed point under coarse-graining. A TOE must explain why many microscopic details wash out — or explain why they don't.

**I6 — Fractal dimensions.** Strange attractors have non-integer Hausdorff dimension. If a TOE's substrate is fractal (as IST claims), it must specify the dimension and its relation to observable quantities.

**I7 — Bifurcation structure.** As parameters vary, systems undergo qualitative transitions (period-doubling, intermittency, crisis). A TOE must predict where these transitions occur or explain why its parameters do not vary.

**From complexity theory:**

**I8 — Emergence.** Macroscopic order can arise from microscopic rules without external control. A TOE must explain how its classical limit emerges, not just assert it.

**I9 — Renormalization.** Physical laws at different scales are related by RG flow. A TOE must have a well-defined RG structure or explain why it doesn't.

**I10 — Criticality.** Many complex systems self-organize to critical points (power laws, scale-free distributions). A TOE must explain why the universe is or is not at criticality.

**I11 — Computational structure.** Some substrates are computationally universal; some are not. A TOE must say what its substrate can and cannot compute, and how this relates to physical observability.

**I12 — Information conservation.** Information is not destroyed (unitarity in QM; black hole information). A TOE must account for information flow, especially at horizons and in collapse.

**I13 — Entropy bounds.** Bekenstein-Hawking, holographic bounds. A TOE must respect or explain these bounds.

**I14 — Thermodynamic behavior.** The arrow of time, the second law, fluctuation theorems. A TOE must derive or explain these, not assume them.

### 1.2 The meta-invariant

There is a deeper pattern: **every invariant is a statement about what survives coarse-graining.** Chaos theory says: the attractor survives, the trajectory does not. Complexity theory says: the universality class survives, the microscopic model does not. Renormalization says: the fixed point survives, the bare parameters do not.

A TOE candidate must say what survives at every scale. If it cannot, it has not identified its invariants.

## Part 2: Principles from the Blind Men and the Elephant

The parable is usually read as "everyone is partially right." The deeper reading is: **the elephant is not the sum of the parts. It is the structure that makes the parts cohere.**

### 2.1 The principles

**P1 — Partial correctness.** No mature candidate is entirely wrong. BM has real trajectories. IST has real arithmetic constraints. Each touches a real feature.

**P2 — Mutual incompatibility at the surface.** Candidates contradict each other's vocabulary. BM says nonlocal; IST says local. These are surface-level contradictions.

**P3 — Deep compatibility is possible.** Two apparently contradictory descriptions can be duals. Wave and particle; position and momentum; electric and magnetic. The contradiction is a failure of the observer, not the object.

**P4 — No totalizing claim.** No candidate can claim to be the whole elephant. Every claim is partial. This is not a weakness — it is the condition of any finite observer.

**P5 — Test by projection.** Each candidate's claims must project onto observables. The projection is the map from the candidate's vocabulary to measurable quantities. If the projection is undefined, the claim is not testable.

**P6 — Structure over substance.** The invariant is the relation between the parts, not the parts themselves. The trunk is not the elephant; the relation between trunk, ear, and tail is.

**P7 — The elephant is not privileged.** There is no "view from nowhere." The elephant itself is a construction of the observers. The deepest invariant is the structure that makes the observations cohere, not a Ding an sich.

### 2.2 How these principles constrain a TOE

A TOE candidate must:

- Acknowledge its own partiality (P4).
- Specify its projection onto observables (P5).
- Show how its vocabulary relates to other candidates' vocabularies (P3).
- Identify the structure that survives translation (P6).
- Not claim to be the final truth (P7).

This is why EBP v2.1's "no final-truth claim" rule is not merely a bureaucratic formality. It is a direct application of P4 and P7. A TOE candidate that claims final truth has misunderstood the elephant.

## Part 3: An AI Evals Framework for TOE Candidates

AI evals are the mature discipline for evaluating systems that cannot be verified by direct proof. TOEs share this condition: they are too large to prove, too speculative to test directly, too interconnected to isolate. The eval framework transfers.

### 3.1 The eval categories

**E1 — Benchmark reproduction.** The candidate must reproduce known physics in known limits. GR in the classical limit. QM in the quantum limit. Standard Model at accessible energies. **Pass condition:** all known results reproduced within declared tolerance.

**E2 — Held-out prediction.** The candidate must predict something not used in its construction. A TOE fit to known physics is not a TOE; it is a curve fit. **Pass condition:** at least one prediction that was not an input.

**E3 — Adversarial evals.** Known no-go theorems must be respected or explicitly violated with explanation. Bell. Coleman-Mandula. Weinberg-Witten. Haag. **Pass condition:** each no-go theorem is either satisfied or explicitly evaded with a stated mechanism.

**E4 — Regression tests.** As new physics is discovered, does the candidate accommodate it? **Pass condition:** each new experimental result can be absorbed without re-deriving the entire theory.

**E5 — Calibration.** Does the candidate's confidence match its status? "Theorem" claims must have proofs. "Conjecture" claims must have stated gaps. **Pass condition:** every claim has an honest status label.

**E6 — Distribution shift.** Does the candidate apply to regimes it was not designed for? Early universe. Black holes. Many-body. **Pass condition:** the candidate either predicts correctly or explicitly states its domain of validity.

**E7 — Ablation.** Does removing a component break the candidate's predictions? **Pass condition:** each component has a stated role; removing it degrades specific predictions.

**E8 — Contamination check.** Was the candidate constructed by fitting to the data it claims to explain? **Pass condition:** the derivation path is documented; inputs and outputs are distinguished.

**E9 — Interpretability.** Can the candidate's claims be inspected? Are the derivations checkable by a third party? **Pass condition:** an independent researcher can verify the key claims without privileged access.

**E10 — Robustness.** Does the candidate survive small changes to its assumptions? **Pass condition:** the predictions are stable under perturbation of parameters, or the parameter values are fixed by a mechanism.

**E11 — Falsifiability.** At least one prediction must be testable. **Pass condition:** a specific experiment or observation could refute the candidate.

**E12 — Kill conditions.** Are the failure modes explicit? **Pass condition:** for each major claim, a stated condition under which the claim would be abandoned.

### 3.2 How to know it holds water

The evals above are necessary but not sufficient. The deeper question is: **what makes the whole set cohere?**

**C1 — Internal consistency.** No two claims contradict each other. Where contradictions appear, they are explicit and have stated resolutions.

**C2 — Parsimony.** Fewer free parameters is better. A candidate with 20 tunable constants is worse than one with 2.

**C3 — Unification.** Explains more with less. A TOE should reduce the number of independent assumptions.

**C4 — Naturalness.** No fine-tuning. Parameters should be fixed by mechanism, not by choice.

**C5 — Cross-checks.** Multiple independent derivations of the same result. If two paths to the same prediction exist and agree, confidence increases.

**C6 — Honest accounting.** Unresolved debt is visible. This is where EBP v2.1 enters: the protocol is not a truth engine, it is an honest-accounting engine. The debt items are the evals.

**C7 — Survival under attack.** The candidate improves under adversarial review, not collapses. This is antifragility in the Taleb sense. A candidate that requires defense is weak; a candidate that generates sharper questions under attack is strong.

The reason this matters: **you cannot evaluate a TOE candidate without a structured framework.** The framework is EBP. The evals are the debt items. The pass condition is promotion.

## Part 5: What This Means for the Program

# Thought Experiment: IST as Particle Nature, BM as Wave Nature

## The proposal

What if the synthesis is not "BM and IST are two descriptions of the same thing," but rather:

- **IST is the particle nature** — discrete, definite, gappy, quantized
- **BM is the wave nature** — continuous, interfering, smooth, guided

And wave-particle duality is not a puzzle within either theory but the structural relation *between* them.

This is a genuinely interesting reframing. Let me explore whether it connects, and where it breaks.

---

## What's compelling about it

**1. The vocabulary aligns naturally.**

IST's vocabulary is discrete: bit strings, permutations, rational constraints, invariant sets, p-adic metrics. Everything is finite, definite, quantized.

BM's vocabulary is continuous: wave functions, guidance equations, quantum potentials, trajectories through continuous configuration space. Everything is smooth, differentiable, interfering.

The surface-level match is striking. IST sounds like particle physics. BM sounds like wave mechanics.

**2. It recovers Bohr's complementarity in a new form.**

Bohr said wave and particle are mutually exclusive descriptions. The proposal says: they are two theories, each capturing one aspect, and the duality is that they are both necessary and neither is complete.

**3. It gives the Projection Problem a new name.**

If IST is particle nature and BM is wave nature, then the map π from IST to BM is the wave-particle duality transformation. This is a reframing of the same problem, but it makes the physical content clearer: what is the rule that converts definite particle-like data into an interfering wave-like description?

**4. It connects to the double-slit.**

- IST: the particle goes through one slit (one bit string, one definite path).
- BM: the wave function goes through both slits and interferes with itself.
- The interference pattern emerges from the statistics of many IST-definite trajectories.

This is actually how BM already describes the double-slit. The proposal is that the "particle" side of this description is IST, not BM's particle positions.

---

## Where it breaks

**1. BM already contains both aspects.**

BM is not just wave mechanics. It has particles with definite positions AND a wave function that guides them. The wave-particle duality is *within* BM, not between BM and something else.

To say "BM is the wave nature" is to ignore that BM's particles are as fundamental to BM as its wave function. You cannot extract BM's wave aspect and leave its particle aspect behind without losing BM.

**2. IST already contains both aspects.**

IST has discrete bit strings (particle-like) AND a chaotic shift map that evolves them (wave-like in the sense of dynamics). It has definite measurement outcomes (particle-like) AND phase interference via the cyclotomic lattice (wave-like).

IST is not purely particle nature. It has its own internal wave-particle structure.

**3. The direction of fundamentality is inverted.**

In BM, the wave function is fundamental and particles are guided by it. The wave is primary; the particle is derivative.

In IST, the discrete substrate is fundamental and the continuum is emergent. The particle is primary; the wave is derivative.

So if you say "IST = particle, BM = wave," you are saying:
- The particle is fundamental (IST)
- The wave is fundamental (BM)
- But they cannot both be fundamental

This is a real tension. The proposal implicitly says one is more fundamental than the other, but does not say which.

**4. The mapping is not one-to-one.**

IST's discrete structures (bit strings, permutations) do not map cleanly to BM's particles (positions in continuous space). IST's continuum-emergence (via coarse-graining) does not map cleanly to BM's wave function (which is fundamental, not emergent).

The thought experiment assumes a correspondence that has not been constructed. This is exactly the Projection Problem in new clothes.

**5. It does not resolve the measurement problem.**

In BM, the measurement problem is solved by definite particle positions. In IST, it is solved by the discrete invariant set. If IST is the particle nature, it should solve the measurement problem — but then BM's particle ontology is redundant. If BM is the wave nature, it should not need to solve the measurement problem — but BM's whole point is that it does.

So the proposal creates a redundancy: two theories, both claiming to solve the measurement problem, and no clear division of labor.

---

## Where it might still connect

Despite the breaks, there are two versions of the thought experiment that could work.

### Version A: The hierarchical version

- **IST is the fundamental particle nature** (discrete, definite).
- **BM is the emergent wave nature** (continuous, effective).
- The wave function in BM is the coarse-grained description of IST's discrete substrate.
- The particles in BM are the coarse-grained description of IST's bit-string states.
- π is the map from IST to BM.

This is coherent, but it demotes BM to an effective theory. BM is no longer fundamental. Its wave function is an emergent object, not a fundamental field.

This is consistent with the two-layer ontology discussed earlier: IST is Layer 1, BM is Layer 2.

### Version B: The dual-aspect version

- **IST and BM are two aspects of the same reality.**
- Neither is more fundamental.
- IST describes the discrete, definite, particle-like aspect.
- BM describes the continuous, interfering, wave-like aspect.
- π is the duality map that relates the two aspects.

This is also coherent, but it requires a third object — the underlying reality that has both aspects — that neither theory describes.

This is harder to defend because it posits something beyond both theories. It is closer to Bohr's complementarity than to a synthesis.

### Which version is better?

Version A is cleaner because it does not require a third object. It says: IST is fundamental, BM is effective, and the wave-particle duality is the relation between fundamental discreteness and emergent continuity.

But Version A has a cost: BM is demoted. Its wave function is not fundamental. Its particles are not fundamental. BM becomes a useful approximation to IST, not a competing theory.

Version B is more symmetric but less tractable. It requires the underlying reality to be described by neither theory, which is a philosophical posit, not a physical one.

---

## Does the thought experiment add anything?

**Yes, in one respect:** it sharpens the question of what π *is*.

If π is just a mathematical projection from IST to BM, it is a technical problem. If π is the wave-particle duality map, it is a physical problem with a long history and a clear conceptual framework.

Framing π as the wave-particle duality map suggests that π should have specific properties:

- It should be non-invertible (many IST states map to one BM description — this is the coarse-graining).
- It should preserve the interference structure (phase relationships must survive).
- It should make definite outcomes emerge from discrete states.
- It should explain why wave and particle descriptions are complementary (why you cannot have both simultaneously).

These are constraints on π that the earlier framing did not state.

**But it also adds a risk:** the framing encourages thinking of IST and BM as *opposite* theories (particle vs. wave) when they are actually *overlapping* theories (both have particle and wave aspects). The overlap is the real problem; the opposition is a simplification.

---

## The deeper question

Bohr's complementarity was not a claim about two theories. It was a claim about two measurement contexts. The wave and particle descriptions are not two things; they are two ways of asking questions, and the answers are mutually exclusive.

The thought experiment changes this: it says the wave and particle are two theories, each with its own ontology. This is a stronger claim than Bohr's.

Is the stronger claim justified? Only if the synthesis can show that IST and BM are genuinely complementary — that they describe the same reality in mutually exclusive but equally valid ways. That is a very strong requirement. It is what Bohr claimed about measurement contexts, but applying it to theories is a much higher bar.

I do not know if that bar can be met. But the thought experiment makes it explicit: the synthesis is not just a mathematical unification. It is a claim about the structure of reality.

---

## Bottom line

The thought experiment connects, but not cleanly. It is a useful reframing that makes the Projection Problem more concrete: π is the wave-particle duality map, and the constraints on π are the constraints on how discreteness becomes continuity.

But it also creates new problems:

- It assigns BM and IST to opposite aspects when both already contain both aspects.
- It either demotes BM to an effective theory or requires a third object beyond both.
- It does not resolve the measurement problem; it duplicates it across two theories.
- It requires π to be non-invertible, phase-preserving, and complementarity-generating — a much richer object than a simple projection.

The most honest version of the thought experiment is Version A: **IST is the fundamental discrete substrate; BM is the emergent continuous description; π is the map between them; wave-particle duality is the structural relation between substrate and description.**

This is coherent. It is a research program. It is not yet a theory. And it still requires the same work as before: build π.

The thought experiment does not change the work. It changes how we talk about it. Whether that is progress depends on whether the new vocabulary generates new mathematics. That is an empirical question about the research process, not a philosophical one.
